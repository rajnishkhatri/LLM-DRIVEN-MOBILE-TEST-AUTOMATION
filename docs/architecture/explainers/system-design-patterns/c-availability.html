System design patterns · Group C · Availability

Four NOs That Keep a Service Up

Availability is not one pattern. It is a stack of admission, isolation, and recovery — four refusals, in order.

Audience Engineering & architecture Reading time ~14 min Status Explainer — catalog group C
How to read the colours: Verified mechanic / default Judgment / trade-off / when-not Operator or product decision Uncertain in the source

A single query that stretches from a few hundred milliseconds to half a minute is enough. The caller’s thread pool fills. Requests queue. Latency walks upstream. A whole service graph degrades — not because every hop is dead, but because one hop never comes back.

Nygard’s electrical metaphor is the right one: trip the circuit so a short does not burn the building. The software version is a stack, not a switch. Timeouts make silence countable. Retries decide whether to try again. The breaker decides whether to call at all. None of those three answers “who may ask,” “how much may run at once,” “what to drop when the box is already full,” or “when this host is dead and another must take its place.”

Availability is a stack of admission, isolation, and recovery: when to refuse work, when to stop calling, when to fail over, and when to do less.

Eleven Concepts sit on that stack. They are not a catalog of peers. They are five questions a request meets, in order. Smash them together and you get the usual production confusion: a 429 treated as a fleet outage, a 503 retried as if it were a blip, a widget omit sold as a failover, a gateway that thinks it is a strangler.

PART ONE · BOUND THE CALL

01Bound the call before you count it

The breaker decides whether to call. Retries decide whether to try again. The timeout decides how long to wait — and the other two depend on it. A hung call that never returns increments no failure counter and holds a worker forever. Nygard lists Timeouts first among the Stability patterns for that reason: a breaker that never sees a failure is not a breaker.

A timeout is a duration. Copied hop-to-hop, it restarts and silently extends the budget. A deadline is a point in time, converted to remaining duration so clock skew does not add time. SRE’s picture: the edge starts at 30 seconds, spends 7, the next hop gets 23, then 19. Without the subtraction, a downstream hop invents a fresh 20 seconds while the caller has 2 left — and does work nobody will read. Five timers share the name; Brooker’s minimum pair is connect + request on every remote call. Idle / between-byte bounds silence (a trickle defeats it). A request bound that includes connect will false-timeout on cold TLS after a deploy.

Verified mechanic · C7

Pick the request timeout from the measured latency distribution at the percentile that matches an acceptable false-timeout rate — p99.9 for a 0.1% budget. Pad when p99.9 sits near p50. The shortest timer on the path is the real SLO, and it is usually one nobody chose. Without an application timeout you inherit the kernel floor: about 127 seconds for a blackholed SYN, about 13–30 minutes for a write to a silently dead peer.

Retries absorb transient faults. Done badly they are the most common sustaining effect in metastable outages — Huang et al. found retry policy in more than half of the studied incidents. Brooker: retries are selfish. Retry connection failures, 408 / 429-with-Retry-After / 502 / 503 / 504, and a timeout under a remaining deadline — the timeout path only if the work is idempotent. Do not retry validation 400s, 401 / 403, spend-cap 429s, cancellation, an Open breaker, or a half-open probe.

Capped exponential backoff without jitter still herds a fleet. Full jitter — random(0, expo(n)) — does the least callee work and is the default for most distributed callers. Backoff helps short spikes, not open-loop overload. A budget caps the fleet: SRE’s three-attempt cap and ~10% retry ratio (about 1.1× load instead of just under 3×). Retry at one layer, typically the one immediately above the failure. Three layers × 4 attempts = 64; five layers × 3 tries = 243×. If Envoy owns retries, the app must not wrap the same call.

Only then does the breaker earn its keep: Closed (count), Open (fail fast — no call reaches the downstream), Half-Open (a limited probe). The caller reclaims threads; the callee gets breathing room. Treat FORCED_OPEN / DISABLED / METRICS_ONLY as kill switches, not happy-path policy. Count connection failures, timeouts, 5xx, and slow-but-successful calls. Do not count 400-class business outcomes. 401 / 403 should alert, not trip. A 429 or 503 that names a delay opens immediately for at least that long, scoped to the quota that was hit — not the whole provider.

Knobs that actually move capacity

The timeout, not the breaker, sets the capacity floor. Eskildsen: a 5-second timeout on a 100 ms worker is about 1/50 of capacity. The breaker stops new calls; threads already parked stay parked. Order the pipeline: .NET runs rate limiter → total timeout → retry → breaker → per-attempt timeout. Resilience4j nests Retry outermost. Retry outside the breaker can be refused by an Open circuit (good). Envoy’s route timeout includes all retries — a 3 s outer with a 2.7 s first try leaves 0.3 s for backoff and the retry. Watch time spent Open, half-open attempts before close, and the spike on close; a breaker without metrics rejects silently.

Trade-off · when-not

A breaker is the wrong tool when the dependency is slower, not down — prefer adaptive concurrency. A retry is the wrong tool for open-loop overload, a non-idempotent write without a key, or an Open circuit. Hedging is the read-path alternative when the replica is slow: backup after expected p95, cancel the loser. gRPC A6 forbids retry and hedge on the same method. Do not hedge a capture.

PART TWO · ADMIT AND PLACE

02Refuse early, then pick a live host

Once a call is bounded, the next refusal is admission. A rate limiter bounds how much a named key may submit per unit time before the work is done. That is policy, not health. The response is 429 — this key exceeded its allocation — optionally with Retry-After. It is not 503, and it is not “the server is doing less per request.”

Keep the three codes honest. 429 means this caller’s quota. 503 means the service is short for everyone (C10). A smaller body, a hidden rail, or already-held stale means the request was accepted and the work shrank (C11). RFC 6585 is explicit that answering every attacker with 429 still costs resources — under attack it may be more appropriate to drop the connection. That escape hatch is shedding, not throttling.

Algorithm family matters less than who owns fairness. Token bucket and meter-form leaky are mirrors; fixed windows herd at the window edge. Stacking a client limiter, a gateway cap, and ten sidecar buckets is N×, not “the” limit. Pick one fairness owner, usually a store keyed by tenant. Envoy’s documented fix for per-instance multiplication is local_cluster_rate_limit.

Verified mechanic · C4 defaults that do not limit

Resilience4j’s RateLimiter default is 50 permits every 500 nanoseconds — about 10⁸ permits per second if left untouched. Envoy’s local token bucket ships with filter_enabled / filter_enforced at 0% and is inert until you raise them. Polly’s and .NET’s “rate limiter” default is 1 000 in-flight permits with queue 0 — a concurrency cap, closer to a bulkhead than a token bucket. Fail-open is the documented default wherever a store is involved: the limiter vanishes under the load you bought it for. Alarm failure_mode_allowed; keep a local backstop that cannot fail open.

Admitted work still has to land somewhere. Load balancing is placement among a live set that health checks have already named. It is not discovery, and it is not key-to-shard routing — that map is Group B’s rebalance / request-routing card (B2). Cite it and stop: a stateless replica may sit behind any peer; a shard can serve a key only on a replica that owns it. A gateway in front is product routing, not a substitute for per-call spreading. The coarsest hop that still multiplexes HTTP/2 wins the imbalance: an L7 façade does not fix an L4 hop behind it.

The gRPC pinning trap is the usual production surprise. HTTP/2 multiplexes many streams on one TCP connection. An L4 hop sees one flow and parks every RPC on one pod. Kubernetes’ kube-proxy is connection-level: no new connections, no opportunity for load balancing. gRPC’s client default is pick_first — all RPCs go to the first READY address until you select round_robin or xDS. Staging with one address hides it. The signature after a rolling deploy is old pods hot, new pods idle. If any L4 hop remains, set MAX_CONNECTION_AGE in minutes — not infinite — with the mandated ±10% jitter.

The north–south façade in front of all of this is an API gateway: TLS, authn, routing, optional aggregation, protocol translation, and Azure’s offloads. A BFF is the same hop, one per user experience, owned by the UI team. A mesh is east–west. A load balancer only spreads. Azure’s absolute rule: business logic never lives in the gateway. ThoughtWorks held “overambitious API gateways” for the same reason. The strangler fig uses the gateway as the usual physical seam; cutover, shadow, dual-write, rollback, and the anticorruption layer belong to B4. Cite that card; do not put the ACL in the gateway.

Trade-off · C6 placement

Put C4 at the edge on the identity you already terminated. Put the outer timeout at the gateway and the per-try timeout in-mesh or in-process. Put failure-trip C1 on the dependency, concurrency C1 on the proxy. AWS API Gateway does not retry integration timeouts — clients must. Kong’s default five retries are transport-only (not 5xx / 429). Stacking Kong 5 × Envoy 2 × SDK 2 on GET is the 243× starter kit. Product defaults are not SLOs: AWS REST 29 s, Kong 60 s, Apigee 55 s / unset Ingress 300 s, APIM 300 s header wait, Envoy Gateway 15 s. Override them. Object-level authorization stays in the service — the gateway cannot know who may touch order 4711.

PART THREE · ISOLATE

03One compartment, one key

The timeout bounds how long a unit is held. The breaker stops new calls once failure is proven. The bulkhead bounds the damage while the breaker is still counting. Partition threads, connections, memory, or whole servers so one dependency, tenant, or request class can exhaust only its own share.

Hystrix’s surviving lesson: a semaphore is not a timeout. Thread-pool isolation runs the call on a dedicated pool; the caller can time out and leave (the pool thread may keep running if the client ignores interrupt). Semaphore isolation is a counter on the calling thread — reject at zero permits, no walk-away. Pair every semaphore with a C7 timeout or the caller still holds the slot for the I/O bound. A single shared outbound pool for “all remote calls” is the anti-pattern the pattern exists to stop.

Envoy’s “circuit breaking” settings on this card are limits — max connections / pending / requests / retries, defaults 1024 / 1024 / 1024 / 3 — not a failure-rate automaton. Overflow is 503 with x-envoy-overloaded. A cluster at 200 rps with 1024 max_requests will not overflow and will not look Open. Istio without a connectionPool stanza is not running those defaults; the knobs sit at 2³²−1. Little’s law sizes the pool: concurrency ≈ throughput × hold-time, from healthy sojourn, not from the timeout. Size to λW, then remember that a hung fraction at T occupies slots the healthy traffic needed — the 20% hang row in the Concept turns a 24-slot, 200 rps pool into a shed of healthy work.

Isolation of effect is the other compartment. Every retry, redelivery, and failover replay asks: what happens if this work runs twice? Exactly-once is at-least-once plus idempotence or deduplication — an effect, not a wire property. HTTP safety is not application idempotency. GET is safe and idempotent; PUT and DELETE are idempotent and unsafe; POST that creates an order is neither. The control is not the method. The control is a key minted once per business operation, before the first attempt, and reused across HTTP retries, queue replays, breaker fallbacks, and workflow activity retries. Minting a key per HTTP attempt is dedup theatre.

The dedup memory must outlive the longest path a duplicate can travel — client retry schedule, queue visibility plus DLQ redrive, operator replay, Brandur’s completer (about 72 hours in that design). Broker windows are a first line only: SQS FIFO’s producer window is a fixed five minutes against default four-day / max fourteen-day retention. After the window, reuse is a new operation. Inbox the business key in the same ACID transaction as the effect; an email server that is not in that transaction will still double-send. Every effect needs its own guard.

Trade-off · when-not

A bulkhead is the wrong tool when you need a priority decision about whom to fail (C10), or when the dependency is hard-down and you want fail-fast plus fallback (C1). Cells as an architecture style belong to E13 — this card only uses a cell as another bulkhead granularity above pools. A key store is the wrong first tool when you needed an atomic increment, causal metadata across replicas, or an outbox so the publish commits with the write. Do not treat the expired IETF Idempotency-Key draft as an RFC; vendors already diverge.

PART FOUR · SURVIVE OVERLOAD

04Drop the request, or do less of it

When offered load exceeds what a server can finish usefully, it has three honest moves: shed (drop some work, cheaply and by priority), push back (make producers slow down), or degrade (do less per accepted request). Everything else is a queue, and a queue only converts overload into latency, memory, and a later bigger failure.

These three are the cuts the Concepts already make. Do not smash them:

  • C4 policy-429. This named key exceeded its allocation. Others may still pass. Headers help honest clients pace.
  • C10 goodput-shedding. The service is short for everyone. Answer 503 (optionally with Retry-After), or refuse the connection. Goodput — work finished without error and inside a latency the client can still use — should plateau as offered load keeps rising. Throughput that includes doomed work is a vanity metric.
  • C11 do-less. The request is accepted. Hide a rail, serve already-held stale, rank against a smaller index, queue a write. The user still gets the core function.

Yanacek: shed early and cheaply; do not fold reject latency into the success histogram; never shed the load-balancer ping — a missed check shrinks the fleet. Drop doomed work (remaining deadline, queue age, LIFO when the protocol allows). Shed on the same CPU target as autoscaling starves the scale-out signal. A static in-flight cap is a bulkhead; adaptive concurrency moves the cap from measured latency. Netflix named the two anti-patterns: no shedding, and congestive failure (the reject path ate the CPU). PlayAPI’s partition: user-initiated = 1.0, pre-fetch = 0.0. Backpressure issues credit when the producer is yours and can wait. For a synchronous RPC whose caller will time out and retry, shed.

Degradation owns the product decision: what the user gets, which dependencies are soft, how stale is sellable, who may flip which switch. REL05-BP01: hard means no substitute — the caller’s availability is the product of its own and the dependency’s. Soft means the caller still performs its core function when the dependency is unhealthy. Ranking that split is a business decision, not an SRE default. An ecommerce landing page can hide recommendations and still show the rest; a static “charged” stub on a consistency core is fraud, not degradation.

Gabrielson’s warning is the failure mode that keeps coming back: an untested second mechanism that dumps load onto a weaker path (cache-miss → database) turned a partial cache outage into a total one. Prefer omit, or serve bytes you already hold. RFC 5861’s stale-if-error hides origin 500 / 502 / 503 / 504 with bytes already in cache. A look-aside miss that multiplies origin load is the opposite. Hystrix is explicit that a cache-via-network fallback lives on a separate bulkhead, else a saturated primary pool blocks the fallback.

Operator / product · kill switch

A kill switch is a long-lived ops toggle that turns a capability off on purpose. C1 FORCED_OPEN / Polly Isolate / Hystrix forceOpen are actuators that stop calling one dependency — they do not hide the widget. Isolating a circuit without an omit still 500s the BFF. LaunchDarkly and Unleash disagree on polarity; pick one convention per fleet. Invert ops switches (Unleash: name disable-recommendations, default false) or fail the SDK default to “feature on.” Never default a kill switch to “feature off” on a core path. A flag flip is not failover (C3) and not a shed of other URLs (C10).

PART FIVE · REPLACE THE DEAD

05Detect, then move — and know what fences the zombie

The breaker decides whether to call. Retries decide whether to try again. Both assume a destination still exists. Failover owns the complementary question: when is that destination declared dead, taken out of rotation, and replaced? Detect-then-move. A health check names unhealth; a routing or promotion action points traffic at a survivor. Automatic where the blast radius is one host; deliberate where it is a region.

Five questions share the name “health check.” Mixing them is how a soft dependency becomes a hard one. Liveness is a process on the port. Local is this box independently broken. Kubernetes startup gates the other two until init finishes. Readiness is whether new work should land here. Dependency / anomaly belongs on a central actor with a rate limit, not on the load-balancer ping. Yanacek’s blank-page fleet still answered pings, failed fast, and a least-requests balancer sent it a disproportionate share.

Fail-open versus fail-closed is a layer property. DNS and LB fail open because an all-fail signal is often a check bug: Route 53 “if no record is healthy, all records are healthy”; ALB/NLB route to all when only-unhealthy targets remain. Readiness and gRPC client health fail closed because a sibling can absorb the traffic. Test partial dependency failure — flapping around the fail-open threshold is worse than a clean all-down.

The first-pass Failover card is superseded here. Outlier ejection is C1 on the data path; brownout of the survivors’ page is C11; least-requests amplification of a black hole is C5; probe timeout as a timer kind is C7. Name what fences the zombie — a DCS lock, a storage epoch, or “we will not promote across this boundary.” A lease without a fencing token is split-brain.

Trade-off · when-not

A health check is the wrong tool for deciding whether to call (C1), bounding how long to wait (C7), shedding load (C10), serving stale (C11), choosing among already-healthy backends (C5), or fencing a zombie writer. Automatic failover is the wrong tool for cross-region promote when the app cannot bear the RTT, for gray failures, and for any path whose fence is “we will reconstruct from backups” — that is restore, not failover. An RTO smaller than detect + TTL is a number you did not choose.

THE SCORECARD

06Which NO, and who owns it

Pulling the stack together: each Concept answers one question, has a small set of operational knobs, and fails in a characteristic way when those knobs are wrong. The pattern is consistent — coordinate the refusals; do not treat any one of them as a complete availability strategy.

◆ Uncertain in the source

07Parked — do not treat as settled

The Concepts themselves mark these as drafts, vendor claims, or unverified strings. They are fenced so a reader does not promote them into knobs.

◆ Uncertain · drafts, not RFCs

IETF Idempotency-Key draft-07 expired 2026-04-18 — vocabulary (400 / 422 / 409), not a standard. Vendors already diverge. Stripe v1 says “errors” on payload mismatch and does not name 422. IETF ratelimit-headers draft-11 is likewise not an RFC; Retry-After still wins when both headers exist, and remaining is advisory.

◆ Uncertain · unverified or vendor-claim

Whether Istio MeshConfig injects retriable-status-codes is unverified in C2. RDS Proxy’s “up to 66%” is a vendor reduction claim, not a measured distribution. Envoy hedge initial_requests is tagged not-implemented. Cloudflare AI Gateway’s page says both “5 retry attempts” and “5 tries.” Patroni maximum_lag_on_failover has no documented default. B4 is cited, not retold.

Refuse the work that would not finish. Stop calling the dest that will not recover. Isolate the blast. Drop what you cannot finish, or do less of what you can. Then — and only then — replace the dead.

If you remember only the thesis, you can retell the group without opening the Concepts: availability is a stack of admission, isolation, and recovery. The four NOs are when to refuse work, when to stop calling, when to fail over, and when to do less. Everything above is the receipts.
