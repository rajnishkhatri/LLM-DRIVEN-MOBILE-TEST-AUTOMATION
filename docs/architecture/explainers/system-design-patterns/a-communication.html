Who Holds the Line

Request/reply, push, callback, or a published contract — the shape you pick decides who waits, who reconnects, and what you can change without a lockstep deploy.

Audience Engineering & architecture Reading time ~14 min Status Explainer — catalog group A
How to read the colours: Verified mechanic / default Judgment / trade-off / when-not Operator or product decision Uncertain in the source

Every service conversation has a shape before it has a product name. Someone asks and waits. Someone pushes on a socket that stays open. Someone knocks later, from the other side of a trust boundary. Someone writes a promise that old and new code must keep at once. That shape — not the logo on the broker, not the version of HTTP — decides who is still on the line when work is slow, what happens when the line drops, and whether tomorrow’s clients can talk to today’s servers without a flag day.

Group A is six patterns that look like a catalog if you walk them in order. They are not six alternatives for the same job. They are four conversation shapes, plus the contract that lets those shapes evolve. Request–response is the default: one caller, one callee, one reply — or a classified failure — that the caller waits for. Everything else in the group is a deviation: a broker that holds work in time, a long-lived push socket, a callback across organisations, or a published surface you can change without redeploying every client.

The shape of the conversation — request/reply, push, callback, or published contract — decides who waits, who reconnects, and what you can change without a flag day.

If you remember only that, the rest of this page is the wiring underneath it: how the bytes actually move, how connections live and die, what a proxy’s idle clock does to a “healthy” stream, and when holding the caller is the wrong shape.

THE CAST

01Four shapes, six characters

Meet them as characters, not a numbered list. The waiter (REST over any HTTP version, and gRPC unary) couples caller and callee in time: one reply, or a classified failure, that the caller waits for. You buy simplicity and consistency; you pay every idle, route, and deadline timer on the path — and a timeout that leaves the outcome unknown. The time-shifter is a channel, not an architecture: a queue, topic, or log holds work while consumers are away and fans one event to many readers. Competing consumers, ordering units, leases, and dead-letter mechanics live here. The event-driven style — topologies, quanta, when the whole system should be event-driven — is Group E.

The open line is two characters on one long-lived hold. A WebSocket is an independent TCP protocol whose only debt to HTTP is the opening handshake; both peers send whenever they like. Server-sent events are server push as an ordinary HTTP response that does not end, with reconnect and a resume cursor built into the browser. One-way text versus true duplex is the fork. The callback inverts request–response across a trust boundary: the producer owns delivery and retries; the receiver owns an internet-facing POST; ack is a status code; authentication is cryptography. The promise is the published contract those conversations carry. Request–response owns the call. The contract owns the shape of that call as it changes.

Verified · A2 is mechanism

Pub/sub and queues on this page are the channel: competing consumers versus fan-out, ordering units, leases, dead-letter mechanics, and what a naive load balancer does to advertised listeners. Whether the system should be event-driven — broker versus mediator, how many quanta — is Group E. Cite it; do not steal its story.

THE HOLD

02Who waits

Start with the waiter: every other shape is a way of refusing to hold the line. HTTP is stateless at the message layer — connection reuse must not change interpretation. Safe methods (GET, HEAD, OPTIONS, TRACE) do not request a state change. Idempotent methods add PUT and DELETE. POST and PATCH are neither. A client should not automatically retry a non-idempotent method unless it has another way to know the work is idempotent; a proxy must not. Treating POST as retryable buys availability and loses exactly-once. RFC 9110 calls the “idle persistent connection closed before any response” guess the riskier path.

How the exchange travels changes what you pay. HTTP/1.1 is practically stop-and-wait per connection. HTTP/2 multiplexes streams and keeps TCP head-of-line: one lost packet stalls every stream. HTTP/3 recovers per stream and negotiates idle timeout in the handshake — clients should open a new connection as that timeout approaches. gRPC unary always travels as HTTP POST with trailers: a well-formed RPC answers HTTP 200 even when grpc-status is not OK. If content-type does not begin application/grpc, servers should answer 415. The default gRPC deadline is none — omit grpc-timeout and the server assumes infinite. DEADLINE_EXCEEDED may follow a successful write. A timeout leaves the outcome unknown; retry without an idempotency key duplicates work; “fail fast” frees the caller and creates orphans.

When the caller should not wait — minutes of work, many consumers of the same fact, a server that speaks after hangup — holding the line is the wrong shape. A queue absorbs a burst (within “queues don’t fix overload”). A socket or SSE keeps a session. A webhook knocks later. The waiter stays the default when the ask/answer fits in a budget tighter than every proxy on the path, and the caller needs the result to continue.

Verified default · hop clocks (2026-09-13)

Envoy route 15 s, HTTP idle 1 h, H2 streams 1024. nginx keepalive 75 s, proxy I/O 60 s, H2 streams 128. ALB idle 60 s, client keepalive 3600 s, target protocol default HTTP/1.1. HAProxy unset timeouts are infinite. Chromium HTTP/1.1 sockets per host: 6. HTTP/2 PING does not reset ALB or Envoy HTTP idle.

THE DROP

03Who reconnects

Reconnect is not one idea. Request–response has no session to resume. After GOAWAY or idle close the client opens a new connection and retries only unstarted or marked-idempotent calls. Already-accepted streams run to completion; cancellation is not a rollback. Coalescing fails as 421 Misdirected Request — retryable even for POST, another duplicate risk if the first hop already applied the write. A proxy must not generate 421.

A WebSocket has no protocol resume either. RFC 6455 has no Last-Event-ID. WHATWG says implement reconnect in onclose; the new object is a new handshake and a new connectionId. Close 1006 — “TCP died with no Close” — is the idle-timeout signature and the one you reconnect; 1000 usually is not. The protocol has no jitter. Missed frames are gone unless the app persisted them. SSE already did that homework: EventSource sends Last-Event-ID when the cursor is non-empty. Blink waits 3 s with no extra jitter; Gecko 5 s (floor 500 ms). A fleet that drops together re-issues in lockstep. Resume is two-sided — if the server never assigned ids, or ignores the header, the cursor is theatre. Empty id: clears it on purpose. A 200 whose MIME is not text/event-stream, or any non-200 (the intro calls out 204), fails the EventSource forever. A gateway 200 + HTML error page kills the widget.

A webhook has no reconnect protocol — each attempt is a new POST, tied by an id header. Schedules are not interchangeable: Stripe live, three days; Slack, three tries (immediate / 1 min / 5 min); Shopify, eight over four hours then subscription delete; GitHub, none (10 s timeout, three-day manual redelivery). On the contract, “reconnect” is client migration. RFC 8594 treats Sunset as a hint; clients that ignore it may simply find the resource gone.

The broker’s reconnect is a fourth dialect. Kafka talks request/response to advertised listeners — a late poll() fails the member (classic: heartbeat 3 s, session 45 s, max poll 5 min). AMQP 0-9-1 multiplexes channels; two missed heartbeats (suggested 60 s) close the TCP connection. Service Bus peek-lock settlement is tied to that receiver — close before settle and you increment toward DLQ at 10. HTTPS pull has no session: a new receive is a new lease. These are not interchangeable “messaging.”

CARDINALITY

04Who fans out

Fan-out is the cardinality hiding inside the shape. Sync request–response has no broker and no competing-consumer partition. A unary handler that issues N downstream calls holds the inbound stream for max(downstream) plus local work — N·M held connections. Many-consumer fan-out belongs on a channel. HTTP/2 collapses Chromium’s six-socket pool, so the cap becomes SETTINGS_MAX_CONCURRENT_STREAMS (nginx/ALB 128; Envoy 1024 since 1.36.0). HTTP/2 at the proxy is not HTTP/2 to the backend: ALB’s target default is still HTTP/1.1. gRPC needs an HTTPS listener and GRPC or HTTP2. ALB has no HTTP/3 listener.

Vendor “exactly-once” words do not mean the same thing. An external side effect is idempotency plus an outbox, not a broker switch. Kafka’s idempotent producer (default on) writes one copy per partition, per producer session; default consume isolation is still read_uncommitted. SQS FIFO collapses producer retries for five minutes and still redelivers on visibility expiry. Pub/Sub’s named exactly-once defaults false; publisher retries mint new ids. RabbitMQ auto-ack is at-most-once; classic DLX republish is too, unless quorum opts in.

On a socket, fan-out means finding the process that owns B’s connection. An in-process map dies with the instance. A registry goes stale when $disconnect is missed (API Gateway’s is best-effort after close). Redis PUBLISH is at-most-once — a down subscriber misses forever. Durable fan-out is a log, not that channel. Autoscaling does not move live sockets; ALB closes the client after deregistration (default 300 s). The live socket is already pinned (L4 tunnel, not a cookie). Cookie stickiness still matters for reconnects and for Engine.IO polling. SSE is one file-descriptor per subscriber; Chromium’s six kNormal sockets are shared across tabs. HTTP/2 removes the client H1 cap, not the server cost. A webhook is N independent POSTs (Stripe caps 16 endpoints; Slack 30,000 events / 60 min). A contract fans out as N majors × M pins — support windows (GitHub ≥ 24 months; Azure preview ≥ 90 days / ≤ 1 year), not a protocol max. Path versioning is the cheap proxy route; header and media-type need Vary or a cache serves the wrong version, or the wrong tenant.

THE PATH

05The clocks and the proxies

The production bug that repeats across this group is a timeout sandwich plus a hop that mutates the conversation it thought it was forwarding. REST-over-HTTP has no remaining-budget header. gRPC converts a deadline to remaining grpc-timeout (Java and Go by default; C++ must enable it). nginx proxy_read_timeout is between I/O ops, not total time — a backend that dribbles a byte every 59 s never hits 60 s. ALB has two clocks: idle 60 s (PING does not reset it) and client keepalive 3600 s from connect. If the app’s idle is shorter, the target closes first and ALB returns 502. The shorter closer always wins. REST clients key off HTTP status; gRPC clients key off trailers — a proxy that only sees :status 200 will not trip on grpc-status: 14. te: trailers is how gRPC detects incompatible proxies.

WebSocket heartbeats live under the shortest hop that sees no bytes. RFC 6455 gives Ping/Pong and no interval; JavaScript cannot send a protocol Ping. Socket.IO v4: ping 25 s, timeout 20 s, client-dead at 45 s — nginx’s 60 s read timeout must be greater than that sum. A 25 s ping clears ALB 60 s; it does not extend API Gateway’s 10 min idle / 2 h cap (close 1001). CloudFront WebSocket is HTTP/1.1 only, idle 10 min. NLB TLS idle is 350 s and not configurable; raise TCP idle above that without matching the ENI and the ENI silently drops state. HTTP/2 WebSocket needs RFC 8441 on every hop; CloudFront does not. Hop-by-hop Upgrade must be re-injected.

SSE has two failures. Buffering (“works on localhost, bursts in prod”) is not fixed by heartbeats — nginx proxy_buffering defaults on; send X-Accel-Buffering: no. Idle teardown closes a quiet stream: ALB/nginx 60 s, Cloudflare origin 125 s → 524, Azure App Gateway 20 s, GCP 30 s as a total deadline. WHATWG comments about every 15 s must be payload bytes. Kafka clients must dial advertised listeners, not a random L4 VIP (broker idle 10 min, client 9 min). RabbitMQ wants activity in the 5–15 s range to satisfy most proxies. Webhook MACs are over the raw body — a pretty-printing proxy or express.json() before the route breaks HMAC. Shopify treats 3xx as failure (an HTTP→HTTPS 301 is an outage). Slack 3 s, GitHub 10 s, Shopify 5 s: an LB shorter than that turns a 2xx into the producer’s timeout.

THE PROMISE

06What you can change without a flag day

An API is a promise to code you cannot redeploy. Byte compatibility is the encoding layer. This group owns the published contract over time. Inside one organisation, servers upgrade first, clients second. Cross-org APIs cannot force that, so versions run side by side.

REST/JSON is compatible by convention: new optional fields in, new response fields out; clients ignore unknowns. Rename, type change, newly required input, enum removal — those are breaks. A read-modify-PUT that drops fields it never understood is silent loss. proto3 is wire-safe when you add a field or reserve a removed number; changing a number, or moving fields into an existing oneof, is not. A field rename is not a protobuf break (identity is the number) and is a break under JSON transcoding — unknowns survive binary and die on JSON. Do not bump package …v2 for a new optional field. GraphQL prefers one evolving schema: add fields, @deprecated them, remove when usage is gone. Making a field non-null or nullable the other way is breaking. That is evolution by selection, not by URL.

Selector misses are the contract’s reconnect errors: Azure omit/unknown api-version → 400; unknown GitHub version → 410; bad Accept → 406; gRPC rename → UNIMPLEMENTED; a removed GraphQL field still queried → validation error. After sunset, RFC 8594 does not specify which status. Pact verifies the current provider against what consumers actually call — { latest: true } is not recommended; mobile uses --all prod. Removing a field nobody consumes verifies green: usage, not schema. Do not version lockstep internals, GraphQL fields, or proto3 additive changes. Stripe 2017: /v2 is almost as painful as re-integrating; users stick; you keep the old surface or cut them off.

A few operator choices the Concepts leave with you, not with a default:

  • Product Thin webhook versus snapshot payload — leak surface versus self-contained delivery; Stripe ships both, chosen per destination.
  • Product API Gateway WebSocket (10 min / 2 h / 128 KB / no binary, managed @connections) versus ALB (you run the process, idle up to 4000 s, native 101) versus NLB (L4, TLS idle stuck at 350 s). Chat product placement stays off this page.
  • Operator Ordering unit on a channel: partition key, MessageGroupId, session, or “unordered is fine.” Extra members past that unit sit idle.
  • Operator Version selector: path (cheap proxy, split identity), required query (Azure — no silent omit), header or account pin (stable URIs, defaults surprise), media type (HATEOAS, Vary), or don’t version (GraphQL / additive proto3).

08What the Concepts refused to invent

Everything above this line is a claim the six Concepts actually make. The items below are marked uncertain, unpublished, or deliberately unfetched in those pages. They are fenced so nobody treats a gap as a default.

◆ Uncertain · A1

gRPC language keepalive and channel reconnect backoff (keepalive_time, permit_without_calls) were not fetched. Do not invent them. Browser-advertised HTTP/2 SETTINGS were not fetched either — Chromium’s 6 is the HTTP/1.1 socket pool, not the H2 stream cap.

◆ Uncertain · A3

Cloudflare proxied WebSocket idle is unpublished. Whether API Gateway counts RFC 6455 Ping as activity, and whether ALB HTTP keepalive caps an upgraded socket, were left out of the card on purpose. Per-host connection counts are not asserted.

◆ Uncertain · A4 / A5

Last-Event-ID is still not CORS-safelisted (whatwg/fetch#1788 had not landed as of the fetch date) — treat the header as an untrusted cursor, never as a capability token. Safari’s Network.framework H1 cap is unverified. Stripe’s per-attempt webhook timeout is unpublished on the primary page; unofficial backoff-step tables and claimed auto-disable triggers were refused.

◆ Uncertain · A2 / A6

KIP-1274 (flip Kafka group.protocol default to consumer) was not shipped on the 4.3 pages. After advertised sunset, RFC 8594 does not specify which status you get. GitHub’s Deprecation is documented as an HTTP-date, not RFC 9745. Google IBV omit behaviour is configuration-dependent — no single Google-wide default. Stripe’s 2026 named majors belong to the changelog, not a frozen default here.

Pick the shape first. The waiter holds the line. The broker holds the work. The open line holds a session. The callback holds only until 2xx, then a schedule. The promise is how those conversations change without a flag day.

Timeouts decide how long to wait. Retries decide whether to try again. Idempotency decides whether a retry is safe. Group A decides whether the caller holds the line — and what “the line” even is.
