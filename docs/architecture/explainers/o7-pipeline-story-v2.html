The Sealed Map

How we teach a computer to test our banking app — and why the smart part happens before the test ever runs.

Audience Engineering & Product Reading time ~10 min Status Explainer — how the o7 pipeline works
How to read the colours: AI / reasoning Deterministic machine Human decision Proposed — not yet decided or designed

Every test is a little journey. You start somewhere, you travel to where you need to be, and once you arrive you check whether the thing you came to see is actually there. It's exactly like using a map: you need to know your starting point, your destination, and the directions in between.

Our mobile test automation pipeline — the one we call o7 — is built on that simple idea. A test of our banking app might say: "send $25 to a saved Zelle recipient and check the confirmation." To run it automatically, the system has to figure out where to start (the login screen), how to get to the Zelle send screen, and what "success" looks like when it lands there.

This is the story of how o7 does that, and — more importantly — of a single design decision that shapes everything else:

o7 splits the work into making the map and driving the map. All the clever AI lives in the map-making. By the time a test actually runs, the map is sealed — and the thing driving it is a machine that never improvises.

If you remember only one thing from this post, make it that. Everything below is the reasoning behind it.

Before we zoom in, here's the whole journey a test takes, start to finish. Read the colour of each stop: amber means AI is doing the thinking, teal means a deterministic machine is doing exact work, and slate means a person decides.

PART ONE · MAKING THE MAP

02Where the AI earns its keep

Here's the thing about a large language model: it already knows how the world works. Ask it to "check an account balance" and it understands, without being told, that a banking app is a locked box — you log in first, you find the accounts area, then you can read a balance. That background knowledge is genuinely useful, and o7 puts it to work in three specific places.

Reading the destination out of a vague note

A manual test is often just a sentence a person typed. The first AI job is to turn "send $25 to a saved Zelle recipient and check the confirmation" into a precise, ordered list of steps — a plan the rest of the pipeline can work with. We call that plan the Test Case IR (think of "IR" as simply the structured recipe).

Crucially, when the AI is unsure — which recipient? which account? — it doesn't quietly guess. It flags the uncertainty as a visible field and hands that decision to a person. That habit runs through the whole design: the AI is allowed to reason in the fog, but the fog is always made visible, never painted over.

Surveying the app to draw a map

The second AI job is exploration — and this is the part your intuition about "discovering places as we go" describes exactly. The system opens the app and, screen by screen, an AI looks at what's on the display and suggests the next move: "to reach Zelle, tap the thing that looks like Payments." A separate, non-AI component actually performs the tap and records what it found.

The screens and the connections between them get saved into a reusable screen map — built once, then reused by every future test, growing over time to cover more and more of the app. That map is the quiet workhorse of the whole pipeline, so it's worth a proper look.

The map itself: a graph of screens

So what is this map, exactly? It's a graph — the same idea as a metro map. Each screen in the app is a stop (a node); each action that moves you from one screen to another — a tap, a swipe, a typed entry — is a line between two stops (an edge). o7 builds this graph once, and every future test rides on it.

Every stop has a fingerprint. A screen isn't remembered by a name that could change — it's remembered by a signature computed from its structure (its heading, its layout shape, its stable element IDs). That's how the machine later knows it has genuinely arrived at "the Zelle send screen," and not somewhere that merely looks similar.

Every line records a cost and a trust level. A connection stores how expensive it is to travel — a deep-link shortcut is cheapest, a tap is mid, typing is dearest — and how much we trust it:

  • A brand-new connection is added as a candidate — seen once, not yet proven.
  • Only after it's confirmed on a second, independent run is it promoted to verified.
  • If a connection later fails — the app changed, a button moved — it's marked broken and quietly routed around. Never silently trusted.

This is why the map keeps getting cheaper to use. The first time o7 needs to reach a screen, it may have to explore (the AI-assisted greedy loop above). But once a route is verified in the graph, reaching that screen again needs no AI at all — it simply replays the known, trusted path. The design target is that roughly 90% of runs take this "already-mapped" fast path, and only genuinely new or changed screens fall back to exploration.

And when the app ships a new version, every route is automatically marked unverified again — so the map re-proves itself rather than trusting stale directions. The safe default.

Where this sits on the honesty line

The screen map is part of o7's map-making design. Like the deep-link loop below, the graph's storage design is worked out but not yet approved — so treat it as a designed direction, not shipped behaviour. What it never does is touch the sealed test run: the machine driver still walks the committed route, step by step.

Some destinations have a secret shortcut

Not every screen has to be reached by tapping through the app. Many banking apps expose deep links — special URLs like erica://zelle/send that jump straight to a screen. When one exists, it's by far the cheapest way to arrive, so o7's map records it as the cheapest possible connection (a "cost-1" edge, versus cost-2 for a tap and cost-3 for typing).

Two things keep this safe and honest. First, o7 only ever discovers deep links the app already supports — an AI may suggest candidate URLs within the app's own scheme, but a deterministic probe must confirm each one truly lands on the target before it's saved. o7 never invents a shortcut. Second, every URL is checked against a strict allowlist three separate times — when it's proposed, again by the component that launches it, and again whenever the map is loaded — so nothing outside the sanctioned list can ever fire.

Where this sits on the honesty line

The cost model (deep link = cheapest edge) and the deny-by-default allowlist are part of the accepted map design. The discover-and-probe loop that actually finds new deep links is a proposed sub-loop — a design that's been worked out but not yet approved — so it's flagged as such below. And note the boundary that matters most: deep links live entirely in map-making. The sealed test run never launches one — the machine driver still walks the committed route step by step.

Writing down how to recognise each landmark

For every step, o7 records how the driver will later recognise the button or field it needs — its "locator." And here the AI is deliberately the last resort, not the first. o7 tries five sources in a fixed order, and only reaches for a model when the reliable, exact methods have all come up short:

PART TWO · THE SEAL

03Sealing the map

Everything so far is fuzzy and changeable — drafts a surveyor can still redraw. Then comes the moment that gives o7 its name and its guarantees: the commit.

The finished recipe and its list of landmarks are locked together and stamped with a fingerprint — a SHA hash we call the irDigest. From this instant, two things are true:

  • The sealed map is the program. There's no separate "turn the plan into code" step. o7 deliberately deleted code-generation. The sealed file itself is what runs.
  • Nothing is fuzzy again. The fingerprint means the exact map that was reviewed is the exact map that runs, character for character — and any report can later be re-run at that fingerprint to reproduce the result.
Why this matters to the bank

Auditability. When a regulator or an internal reviewer asks "what exactly did this test do, and can you prove it?", the answer is a fingerprint that pins the precise recipe and the precise version of the machine that ran it. You can re-run history and get the same answer — because no AI was in the loop when it ran.

PART THREE · DRIVING THE MAP

04The machine driver never improvises

Now the map is sealed, and the guiding rule flips: anything that must be correct, cheap, and repeatable should never depend on a model's live output. So from here on, it's all deterministic machinery.

A checklist before any phone is touched

Before spending a single minute of real device time, a deterministic gate runs seven checks on the sealed map — like a pre-flight checklist. Does the recipe parse? Are all the moves legal moves? Does every step have a time limit (no waiting forever)? Is every landmark on the recognition sheet? Are there any secrets accidentally written in plain text (there must not be)? Any leftover ambiguity? And finally, a dry run — a no-device walk through the whole route on paper. It costs nothing, and it's the safety net that lets us trust the pipeline without a human re-reviewing every test.

Dispatch — exactly once

An approved test is dropped into a queue built so that even if the order is accidentally submitted twice, exactly one run happens and we never double-charge our device budget.

The core: following the sealed route on a real device

This is the heart of o7. A version-pinned interpreter — itself a fixed, audited piece of software with its own fingerprint — picks up a real phone from our device cloud and walks the sealed recipe one step at a time, using the industry-standard Appium toolkit to actually tap and type. For each step it:

  • tries the recorded landmarks in the exact sealed order (first the primary ID, then the one committed backup);
  • respects each step's own time limit and "wait for the screen to settle" instruction;
  • and never searches for, invents, or "heals" a landmark on the fly.

If the recorded landmarks don't match, the test fails hard — a red build — and stops. It does not go looking for a workaround.

A subtle but important safeguard

Modern device clouds (Perfecto, BrowserStack and the like) now quietly inject their own AI "self-healing" into test runs. o7 explicitly switches that off and records that it was off — because a cloud silently "fixing" a broken locator would destroy the very repeatability the seal was meant to guarantee. If it can't confirm the cloud AI is disabled, it quarantines the run.

Reason-code, record, and a human signature

When a run ends, a deterministic lookup stamps it with one of exactly seven standard reason-codes (locator not found, timeout, assertion mismatch, app crash, and so on) — a pre-printed list, not a free-form AI narrative. The result is filed as a report pinned to the exact fingerprints. And the final certification is a named person's decision, made after the run. The machine's clean pass is only a precondition; fully-automated sign-off is deliberately not allowed. Accountability stays with a human.

◆ Proposed — not yet decided or designed

06Ideas we could add (clearly labelled)

Everything above this line describes o7 as it is specified today. What follows is a set of enhancement ideas — genuinely useful, and a natural fit for the design, but not yet decided, designed, or built. No decision has been taken on any of them. We're keeping them visibly fenced off so nobody mistakes an idea for a plan.

There's one hard rule on all of them: they belong to map-making only. None of them may touch the sealed replay walk — that has to stay a plain, no-search, no-improvisation drive.

◆ Proposed · highest value

Smart shortest-path routing (Dijkstra). Our screen map already stores a "cost" on every connection (a deep link is cheaper than a tap, which is cheaper than typing). Today nothing uses those costs. A classic shortest-path algorithm would turn them into an optimal route-planner at map-making time — producing shorter, cheaper, more robust test routes automatically. Because the cost data is already part of the design, this is the most natural fit of the ideas here — but it is still just an idea.

◆ Proposed · designed, not yet approved

The deep-link discovery sub-loop. A small loop that finds new shortcuts: an AI suggests candidate deep-link URLs within the app's real scheme, a deterministic probe confirms each one lands on the right screen, and only confirmed links are saved to the map as cost-1 edges. The safety design around it (discovered-not-invented, and a deny-by-default allowlist re-checked three times) is settled; the loop itself has an accepted design but is not yet ratified. It would only ever add shortcuts to the map — never launch one during a sealed test run.

◆ Proposed · depends on the two above

Prefer shortcuts when routing. Once shortest-path routing and the deep-link sub-loop both exist, route-planning would automatically favour a cost-1 deep-link teleport over a long tap-by-tap path — making generated test routes dramatically shorter and less fragile. This one stacks on the two ideas above, so it is the furthest from decided.

◆ Proposed

Faster routing on big apps (A*). The same route-planning as above, but with a smart hint to skip obviously-wrong directions — useful once the app map gets large. Weaker footing than Dijkstra (we'd need to design the hint), but a natural next step.

◆ Proposed

Remember-the-best-route cache (dynamic programming). The screen map already behaves like an informal cache of known routes. Formalising it — storing the best-known path for each start-and-goal pair — would make "reuse the saved route vs. explore again" a principled, deterministic decision instead of a happy accident.

◆ Proposed

Guaranteed-coverage exploration (BFS/DFS). Today's explorer is "greedy" and can get stuck on hard screens, burning its budget. A systematic sweep would instead guarantee every reachable screen gets mapped — trading some AI flexibility for completeness. Worth it for a push toward that 90%-plus coverage goal.

◆ Proposed

Stronger dry-run checking (dependency ordering). The pre-flight "dry run" could be sharpened to formally prove no step depends on a later one — catching impossible orderings on paper, before any device is touched.

◆ Proposed

Auto-tidy before sealing (critic + bounded repair). A deterministic checker that fixes small mechanical defects in a recipe (a missing time limit, an unused landmark) before it's sealed — reducing checklist rejections. Strictly upstream of the seal: the running test still gets no self-repair whatsoever.

The AI figures out the route. The machine drives it, exactly, every time. That separation is the whole idea — and it's what lets a bank trust an automated test.

07Where the coverage comes from

Back to the original bet. How does o7 expect to cover more than 90% of an app's navigation? Not from any single trick, but from four sources stacking up in the screen map:

  • The AI's world knowledge handles the typical, "any banking app works like this" cases — roughly 60–70% on its own.
  • The steps people already wrote in their manual tests feed straight into the map.
  • Greedy exploration discovers the in-between screens as it goes and saves them.
  • And every run adds more — the map only grows.

Each source covers what the others miss. Together, the hypothesis is that they clear 90%. And whatever the AI can't figure out, a human fills in — visibly, by design, never silently.
