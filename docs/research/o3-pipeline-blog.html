The Bridge Pipeline — Adaptability with a Critic

How the hybrid keeps demo-speed interpretation alive — and still refuses to print a silent green when a heal goes wrong.

~10-minute read · August 2026

"Show me the executable that produced this verdict, and prove it hasn't changed."

Ask that of O3 — the proposed hybrid — and the honest answer is conditional: not yet, not while the run is still interpreted. The report carries an auditCaveat that says as much. Export the successful path into committed code, re-enter a static gate, and the auditor's question becomes answerable. Until then, you have a gated demo lane — not a certified one.

That is the hybrid promise in one breath: keep the interpreter's adaptability so a POC can move at demo speed, and add a deterministic critic so a wrong heal cannot become a clean PASS. O3 is the bridge. It is not the cautionary villain (that is O2), and it is not the production north-star (that is the O1 spine).

We didn't start with O3

Six architectures went through a weighted bake-off for a bank shipping a monthly app release: the LLM-free spine (O1), a pure runtime interpreter (O2), a hybrid (O3), a vendor tool, a YAML-DSL approach, and Meta's regenerate-and-filter pattern. Auditability took 20% of the weight; maintainability was elevated to 22%. The scores are a draft for architecture-board sign-off, using the analyst's weights — not a settled ruling.

O3 lands at roughly 74 out of 100 — close enough to the first-pass leaders to stay in the room, and the comparison's verdict card names it the POC / spike pick. Its SWOT is the bridge in miniature: interpreter adaptability plus a deterministic critic; export-on-success as the audit bridge; two modes to build; interpreted runs not audit-grade until materialized; scope-creep risk if critic + repair + export bloats a throwaway. Production posture stays with the evolved O1 spine and regenerate-and-filter maintenance — O3 proves the adaptability needle without pretending to replace the commit-pinned substrate.

The hybrid trick

Here is the whole idea, stated once. O3 runs in two modes that share one IR shape. Interpret executes TestCaseIR live on a device — adaptive, demo-friendly, and gated by an embedded healPolicy so heals fail closed when confidence, path criticality, or corroboration say no. Export-on-success materializes a surviving path toward committed code, so the run can eventually re-enter a static gate and earn a real codeCommit. The mock suite's README puts the artifact contrast cleanly: two artifacts, but the IR is fatter — discipline lives inside the IR itself, not across a four-file spine.

That is a bridge, not a firewall. O1's commit wall keeps every LLM on the authoring side of git. O3 lets interpretation happen in the run — and then insists that certification wait for materialization. The comparison's tension note is blunt on who can answer for a verdict: O1, O5, and O6 via committed artifacts; O3 and O4 only after export; O2 not at all.

One test's journey

Follow ACC-1042 — "Login with valid credentials shows welcome" — the same login flow every option in the mock suite renders. Under O3 the IR is still the live action script, but it is no longer a bare list of steps. Credentials remain vault references (vault:user_qa / vault:pass_qa). Platforms, provenance, and an irDigest travel with the file. Each step carries a timeout and a sync wait — ELEMENT_PRESENT, CLICKABLE, TEXT_PRESENT — so the execution contract is thicker than O2's thin interpreter loop.

The load-bearing addition is the control surface at the top of the IR:

From TestCaseIR.runtime.json — healPolicy

"healPolicy": {
  "retryBudgetPerStep": 3,
  "healOnMiss": ["LOCATOR_NOT_FOUND", "STALE_ELEMENT"],
  "neverHeal": ["ASSERTION_MISMATCH", "APP_CRASH", "ENV_INFRA"],
  "healConfidenceFloor": 0.90,
  "humanConfirmOnCriticalPaths": ["payments", "auth", "balance"],
  "fallbackToVLM": true,
  "corroborateAgainstScreenshot": true
}

At runtime the interpreter walks the six steps on a real device — the mock pins Appium 2.5.1, XCUITest 5.12, and an iPhone 15 on iOS 17.4 — and every locator miss must clear that policy before it can rewrite the path. The mock run does three attempts (runs: 3, passes: 2) and records flakiness as FLAKY rather than pretending a single green settles the matter. Export-on-success is the optional seventh stop: materialize only what survived, then aim that residue at a static gate. Until that export lands, codeCommit and staticGate stay null by construction — honesty encoded in the report shape.

The heal that didn't pass quietly

What O2 did with the same miss

On the pure-interpreter path, step 4 healed to XPATH://XCUIElementTypeButton[1] at confidence 0.41 — no floor, no screenshot corroboration, no human confirm, no never-heal guard — and the verdict stayed a clean PASS with codeCommit: null. That is the silent-wrong-heal case the risk register labels Critical. O3's point is not that interpretation is safe; it is that the same interpreter loop can refuse to launder a weak heal into green.

Practitioner audits report that ML element-match healing carries roughly 3× the false-pass rate of simpler fallback strategies, and that around 60% of teams disabled AI self-heal within three months — reported industry figures, not this project's measurements. Auto-heal is survivable only with a deterministic critic, human confirm on critical paths, and an audit trail — which is why the comparison recommends O3 for the POC, not O2.

What's in the IR that O2 lacks

The mock suite README says O2 has the fewest artifacts; the controls that make O3 survivable are absent — the danger is in what's missing. O3 keeps the two-artifact footprint and pushes the missing discipline into the IR. Beyond healPolicy, the runtime contract adds per-step timeoutMs and sync waits, an irDigest labelled as the interim pin (the digest note says null/absent is never valid), and report fields that record blocked heals as lineage events rather than disposable log lines.

The risk register names the wounds those fields close. R1 — silent wrong-heal — wants a confidence floor at or above 0.90, alert on every heal, and human confirm on payments, auth, and balance. R2 — non-deterministic verdict — wants a deterministic critic gating acceptance, and materialization before any compliance verdict. R3 — un-audited change — wants heal events on a hash-chain lineage and a materialized diff as the reviewable residue. O3's mock is those mitigations made readable in JSON.

The honesty boundary

The O3 report states the boundary in the artifact itself — not in a slide footnote:

From ReplayReport.json — auditCaveat

"auditCaveat": "Interpreted run — not a certified verdict. Materialize to committed code (export-on-success) and re-enter the spine's static gate before any compliance use."

Interpreted ≠ certified

A gated heal and a pending-confirm verdict are necessary for a responsible POC. They are not sufficient for a bank auditor. Until export materializes the path and a static gate pins a commit, the run remains an interpreted signal — useful for learning which steps flake, heal, or need richer sync, unfit as a compliance verdict.

The SWOT weaknesses are the price of the bridge. Two modes to build — interpret and export — means more moving parts than O2's seductive thin loop. The interpreted mode is not audit-grade until materialized, so every demo must keep that caveat in the room. And the threat column is scope creep: critic + repair + export can bloat a throwaway unless the team treats O3 as a time-boxed spike with a deliberate exit into the O1 substrate. Discipline is part of the architecture, not a soft skill bolted on later.

Economics & the flywheel

On the comparison's per-release TCO table, O3 sits with the regenerate-and-review economics band — roughly $8k–14k a year for a hundred-test suite under monthly releases (O3's own row is about $8k–13k; neighboring O1/O6 rows fill the same band). Human hours land around five to eight per release of supervision, not twenty-plus of hand-editing. The figures are illustrative order-of-magnitude constants from the analyst's draft, pending ARB ratification against bank actuals — not measured spend.

What matters structurally is the residue. O2's flywheel grade is largely wasted: runtime heals are ephemeral; there is often no durable, reviewable patch for the next release to learn from. O3's maintenance model is materialize-and-gate: export plus critic classifications become exemplars and labelled outcomes; repair feedback is few-shot data. The comparison grades that compounding path High — the same family of advantage O6 gets from regenerate-and-filter. You convert engineer hours into compute and leave something the next month can use.

Self-heal remains a narrow tool even when gated. The comparison notes that healing addresses only the roughly 25–30% of failures that are broken locators, at about 75% success on that slice — around 20% of all failures at best. O3's job is not to romanticize heal rates; it is to keep the useful slice while refusing the silent-green remainder.

Where this goes

The comparison's decision framing is two questions with two winners. The POC question picks the proposed hybrid: a runtime-interpreting Appium path with a deterministic critic and bounded repair, materializing successful runs as the bridge toward production. The production question still lands on the evolved O1 spine — LLM-free deterministic replay as the audit-ready foundation, with regenerate-and-filter (never hand-maintain) as the maintenance model once ADR 0014's authoring arm is in place.

Read the path as a migration, not a rivalry. Spike O3 to prove IR-driven adaptability under a real critic. Harvest heal logs, flakiness signals, and export shapes. Carry what worked into Phase 1 as a bridge — then stop pretending the interpreted lane is the certified one. Production certification stays on committed artifacts and an LLM-free spine. That is how a 74-scoring hybrid earns its keep without becoming a permanent second production system.
